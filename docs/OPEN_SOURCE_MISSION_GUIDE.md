# Open-Source Mission Guide

## What is a mission?

An open-source mission is a validated community problem prepared for
collaborative solution work. It is not a promise that software is the answer or
that a proposed solution will be adopted.

## Mission readiness

A mission should include:

- The validated problem statement and affected users.
- Approved evidence and known limitations.
- Community context and constraints.
- Desired outcomes and measurable success criteria.
- Open research questions.
- Privacy, accessibility, safety, and operational considerations.
- A community representative or steward where available.

## Roles

- **Mission steward:** keeps context accurate and coordinates decisions.
- **Community representative:** brings affected-user needs and feedback.
- **Solution proposer:** describes an approach, assumptions, and expected outcome.
- **Maintainer:** governs a linked solution repository and reviews contributions.
- **Contributor:** works on research, design, code, documentation, testing, or rollout.
- **Reviewer/tester:** evaluates quality, safety, accessibility, and community fit.

One person may hold multiple roles, but conflicts should be disclosed.

## Lifecycle

1. **Ready:** the validated problem is documented as a mission.
2. **Exploring:** contributors investigate approaches and unanswered questions.
3. **Proposed:** one or more solution proposals are available for review.
4. **Accepted:** humans approve an approach for implementation; alternatives may remain.
5. **In Development:** linked work is active and milestones are visible.
6. **Testing:** affected users and reviewers evaluate the solution.
7. **Implemented:** delivery evidence exists.
8. **Impact Assessed:** outcomes and limitations have been reviewed.
9. **Paused or Withdrawn:** work stopped with a recorded reason.

## Contribution workflow

1. Read the mission, evidence restrictions, and existing proposals.
2. Join or propose a solution instead of opening duplicate work.
3. Agree on repository ownership, license, roles, milestones, and success criteria.
4. Break work into small issues with acceptance criteria and appropriate labels.
5. Link commits and pull requests to issues and mission milestones.
6. Require review, tests, accessibility checks, and security checks before merging.
7. Publish progress and request community feedback at meaningful milestones.
8. Record implementation and impact evidence without overstating results.

## Recognition

Credit reporters, evidence contributors, designers, researchers, testers,
maintainers, and implementers. Recognition should reflect reviewed quality and
outcomes—not raw activity, votes, commits, or generated code volume.
